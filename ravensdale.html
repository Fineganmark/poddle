Stop 8 of 18 · Kimmage Cross Roads

Ravensdale and Poddle Park

A valley of mills, and the scheme’s main flood storage in the city.

In brief

A valley of old mills, and now the scheme’s main flood storage within the city.

Mill country

Between Kimmage Cross Roads and Sundrive Road the Poddle was once lined with mills. The first Ordnance Survey maps of the 1830s and 1840s show Ravensdale Mills, a corn and flour mill with its own pond, sluice and weir on Kimmage Road Lower, where Ravensdale Park is today. It was still marked on the map in 1940.

Downstream, Larkfield Mills and its pond stood where Sundrive Shopping Centre is now, and another mill and pond sat beside the river near today’s St Martin’s Drive, with a windmill nearby on the same old mill-pond ground. The stump of a windmill survives in St Martin’s Park. At Larkfield, the Plunkett family’s mill and farm later served as a store for weapons landed in the Howth gun-running of 1914.

Near the crossroads, Farrington’s flour mill ran a water turbine into the 1920s; Mr Farrington is remembered as the first person in the area with electric light, until the ESB arrived and he had to stop.

St Anne’s and Brooklawn

Past Kimmage Manor the river runs underground through St Anne’s, comes up near the crossroads, dives under the junction and emerges again in Ravensdale Park. It once flowed through the grounds of Brooklawn, a large house beside the crossroads, and through the St Anne’s estate, where its banks stood fifteen to twenty feet high.

When developers proposed flats on the St Anne’s lands in the early 1970s, with the river to be culverted, the plans were opposed. Dublin Corporation and Dublin County Council refused one scheme for 108 flats on 10 January 1974, citing the river and the land’s zoning as open space.

Flood works

This stretch is central to the River Poddle Flood Alleviation Scheme. Ravensdale Park is being reshaped to store flood water, with new flood walls and a replacement footbridge. Poddle Park gets walls, a sloped path and a new pedestrian bridge, and a flood wall is planned at the end of St Martin’s Drive. Behind Grosvenor Court the river is being realigned with embankments on both sides. Works began in Ravensdale Park in 2025.

Kimmage Cross Roads

The big houses around the crossroads are remembered by Finola Watchorn in Crumlin and the Way it Was. Kimmage Lodge, a large Georgian house behind today’s Glenanne apartments, was the home of the Davy family; the Rev. Humphrey Davy was rector of St Mary’s, Crumlin, from 1867 to 1913. Wainsfort and Fortfield Lodge stood on Fortfield Road, and Fortfield Lodge marked the edge of Kimmage, which ran from Harold’s Cross Green up to it. Her grandfather, P. J. Watchorn, built the KCR petrol station in the early 1930s.

The Glenanne Sports Club, formed in 1943 from the Graftonians, played on the grounds of St Anne’s, a small estate running from the crossroads up to the back of Kimmage Manor. The Poddle ran through it between banks 15 to 20 feet high, which gave the place the look of a glen and the club its name. The club lost its grounds in 1970, when the land was sold for building.

Ravensdale Mill

Ravensdale Mill, also called the Tinker flour mill, stood at the corner of Kimmage Road Lower and Captain’s Lane, now Captain’s Road, with its entrance on Captain’s Lane. Pat Johnson of the Old Dublin Society remembered its iron wheel, about eight feet across, turned by the Poddle, driving the mill’s three floors through gears and a vertical shaft; the millstone was still intact in 1928. The Drake family ran the mill and lived in a two-storey house nearby. Milling stopped around 1917. From 1924 Joe P. Kelly and his three sons made furniture there until the business failed in 1928. By the 1940s little was left. Finola Watchorn played in the ruins on the way home from school, and Lizzie Moran kept a small sweet shop in part of them.

Source: Finola Watchorn, Crumlin and the Way it Was.

Poddle Park and St Martin’s

Between Ravensdale Park and Bangor Road the river gives its name to the streets: Poddle Park, Poddle Green, Poddle View and Poddle Close. The Poddle Park cottages were built for labourers in the 1920s and 1930s, St Martin’s Park and Drive in the 1960s, the Riverside apartments in the 2000s on the site of Brennan’s bakery, Poddle Close on the back gardens of the Clonard Road cottages, and Poddle Green and Poddle View on the grounds of Waterpark House.

In 1967 Dublin Corporation laid out St Martin’s on Martin’s Lands and realigned, deepened and regraded the river to cut flooding. The stretch from Ravensdale Park to Poddle Park was put in a culvert and the river was turned under the Poddle Park road. The stepping stones at the top of Bangor Road, a shortcut for hundreds of factory workers, gave way to a concrete and steel footbridge, which vandals wrecked in 1968.

Local people have long looked after this stretch. In 1973 a city commissioner, George Butler, asked for action on rubbish dumped in the river at St Martin’s Drive and on oil pollution that kept appearing at weekends. In September 1974 the St Martin’s Residents’ Association and Dublin Corporation cleaned the river together, and the association drew up a ten-point plan: notices against dumping, a safe bridge at Bangor Road, an end to oil from local industry, and trees and flowers on the banks.

The floods kept coming. In October 2002 Mary Byrne, then 84, spent the night with her family fighting 18 inches of water in her kitchen in Poddle Park, as she had in the 1986 storm. A new footbridge upstream of the old one was proposed in 2004, and the current flood scheme adds a 1.5-metre wall in Poddle Park.
